# Michael Levin redefining the rules of life, with Bernardo Kastrup

## In one paragraph

Levin and the philosopher Bernardo Kastrup talk about the genome as a set of prompts rather than a blueprint, where the intelligence of cells comes from, and whether the physical world is causally closed. Levin says plainly that he does not think it is, that physicalism is not viable, and that for practical work he uses a two-realm, interactionist model in which patterns from a Platonic space have causal effects. Kastrup argues that no one can claim closure because we have no first-principles physical model of anything bigger than a small molecule. Levin then poses his "experience or memory" choice to Kastrup and runs through new lab work: a cell-training platform for drug conditioning, the Mombot robot, "home worlds" for xenobots and anthrobots, and the boredom theory of aging.

## Main ideas (plain words)

- **The genome as prompts.** Levin describes a recent paper in *Trends in Genetics* that treats the genome as a set of prompts interpreted by the intelligence of cells building a body. He says that five years ago he had to phrase such things very differently to publish them in mainstream journals.
- **An assumption, not a result.** The rule that developmental biology must be explained by chemistry in which nothing knows anything is, he says, an axiom, not a finding. The mapping from genome to body is an intelligent, improvising process: it has goals, learns, routes around obstacles, and finds new goals if blocked.
- **Where the intelligence comes from.** His deeper answer is the same as for the truths of number theory: a Platonic space, possibly a different layer of it.
- **Consciousness first, two realms for now.** Asked about Penrose's three worlds, he says that ultimately he thinks consciousness, or mind, is primary, but in his day job he works with two realms that need different methods and interact. "I don't believe it's causally closed," he says of the physical world, and he thinks things get strange well before quantum mechanics is needed.
- **Kastrup on closure.** Kastrup argues that the computational cost of first-principles models grows so fast that claiming the physical world is causally closed is a belief, not a substantiated scientific statement, and that engineering succeeds by isolating what we do not understand rather than by understanding it.
- **Why questions end in the math department.** Levin's example: cicadas emerge at 13 and 17 years to avoid predators; why those numbers work comes down to primes, a fact physics cannot change. He adds that the same space holds higher-agency patterns that behavioral scientists would recognize.
- **Random analog circuits.** Unpublished work, which he says is not peer-reviewed: in simulations of random analog electrical circuits, making them do standard logic costs them their trainability and memory, and letting them behave freely costs clean computation.
- **Experience or memory?** Levin asks whether you would rather have a wonderful experience and then lose all memory of it, or have the memory without the experience. Kastrup chooses the memory at once. Levin says Kastrup is the first person he has asked who answered immediately. Their talk moves to anesthesia; Levin says his lab is investigating whether stress responses in tissues reveal something the patient's report cannot.
- **New lab work.** An open-source closed-loop platform to train cells, aiming at drug conditioning, where pairing a strong drug with an inert substance might later let the inert one work like "a molecular placebo"; Mombot, "a robot for building biobots", which he describes three ways (a communication tool, an agent exploring anatomical space through frog cells, a prosthetic for the cells); and a pipeline that infers the "home world" xenobots and anthrobots would have evolved in.
- **Aging.** He says the lab sees different bioelectrics in aging and non-aging hydra and in human cells in culture, and now sees the patterns become fuzzier with age. He repeats the boredom theory of aging from a model with no noise and no programmed aging, and calls where the boredom comes from "a totally open question". Asked about very long lives, he speculates about radical changes of body plan and then says "I'm making that up."

## Why it matters

Levin states his metaphysical position plainly here: mind as ultimately primary, physicalism as not viable, and an interactionist two-realm model as his working tool. Next to Kastrup, it shows where a working biologist and a philosopher of mind agree and where Levin holds back for practical reasons.
