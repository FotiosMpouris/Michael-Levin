# Where Does Genius Come From? (Human Potential Lab, with Scott Barry Kaufman)

- **Date published:** 2026-10-28
- **Channel:** Human Potential Lab (not the Levin lab channel)
- **Speakers:** Michael Levin, interviewed by psychologist Scott Barry Kaufman, the show's host
- **Format:** Interview (1 h 2 min)
- **Watch:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XTL9PxDm5B8
- **Topics:** [[platonic-space-and-ingressing-minds|Platonic space and ingressing minds]], [[bioelectricity|Bioelectricity]], [[cognition-in-cells|Cognition in cells]], [[mind-body|Mind, body and brain]], [[ai-and-biology|AI and biology]]

## In one paragraph

Kaufman, who studies human intelligence and creativity, sets out to map his field onto Levin's. Levin defines intelligence for experimental purposes as problem-solving competence, argues it is a spectrum that is an empirical question for each system, and doubts claims about "unconscious" processes because they rest on human verbal reports. He explains bioelectricity as the "cognitive glue" that lets cells pursue large anatomical goals. The second half turns to genius: Levin's "free lunch" accounting, xenobots and anthrobots, a spectrum of inspiration from step-by-step problem solving to Ramanujan, and his Platonic space hypothesis, in which bodies and machines are interfaces rather than makers of minds.

## Main ideas (plain words)

- **A working definition.** For experiments, intelligence is problem-solving: degrees of ingenuity in reaching goals as circumstances change. He says this leaves out play, exploration and creativity, which are harder to quantify.
- **Two entry criteria for the spectrum.** Some goal-directedness, and needing more than the immediate pushes and pulls to predict or relate to the system. The size of the biggest goal a system can pursue is its [[cognitive-lightcone|cognitive light cone]]. He suggests least-action laws give even particles a sliver of goal-directedness, citing a conversation with Chris Fields.
- **Intelligence is not consciousness.** He says he has said little about consciousness so far, that many people "working on consciousness" are really working on behavior or physiology, and that judgments about unconscious processing are calibrated on one talking part of one kind of being.
- **The body as an ecosystem of intelligences.** Cells navigate transcriptional, metabolic and physiological spaces. If we could feel our blood chemistry, he suggests, we would easily see our liver and kidneys as problem-solving agents.
- **Bioelectricity as cognitive glue.** The same tools work on brains and non-brain tissue because every cell has ion channels. Electrical networks let groups of cells hold goals single cells cannot, such as the shape of a limb. His lab built tools to read these patterns (the "electric face") and to write new ones, such as two-headed flatworms. He is explicit that in vivo work so far is in animal models; human work is in vitro only.
- **Free lunches.** Design, evolution and learning are the three ways we "pay" for competence. The gap between effort put in and competence out is the size of the discount. Xenobots and anthrobots, and simple deterministic algorithms such as bubble sort, show competencies none of the three explain.
- **A spectrum of inspiration.** Step-by-step problem solving, then von Neumann jumping steps, then Ramanujan saying a goddess gave him theorems. Levin calls the unexplained arrivals "ingressions" and says the aim is to make them stop being surprises.
- **Platonic space.** Mathematical facts (the digits of e, which polynomial equations have formulas) are not set by physics yet shape physics and biology. His addition: the space may also hold dynamic patterns that behavioral scientists would recognize, even kinds of minds. "I don't think you make them. I think what you make are interfaces."
- **Genius, fate and genes.** He feels he is discovering rather than inventing, and works to make his lab a "lightning rod" for ideas. He reads fate as large attractors in the landscape of possibilities that take effort to escape. Asked about "genetic memory" as an explanation for savants, he says the emphasis on genes is misplaced: one-headed and two-headed flatworms have the same genome.
- **AI.** He thinks the language interface is what we force AI to do and may not be the interesting part of its cognition, and recalls that Turing's paper on the imitation game assumed computers could not do something humans could.

## Why it matters

It is a plain-language walk-through of the free-lunch and Platonic space argument for a psychology audience, and it ties the hypothesis to human creativity, savants and genius, with a researcher of human intelligence asking follow-ups and adding cases from his own field. It is a useful companion to [[platonic-space]] and [[basal-cognition]].
