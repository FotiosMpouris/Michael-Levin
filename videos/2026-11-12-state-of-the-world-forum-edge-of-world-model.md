## In one paragraph

Speaking without slides, Levin lays out his program for a general audience: diverse intelligence studied by experiment, not by redefining words; "mind blindness" as a central problem for humanity; and the claim that he does not believe in dead matter. He describes the regenerative medicine work (asking tissues to build eyes and limbs, normalizing tumors, now aging) as communication with a material that has its own goals. With physicist Anita Goel he discusses whether the origin of life was random, and he answers that the mathematics guiding early molecular networks is already biased toward learning and causal emergence. Asked about latent space, he says bodies and machines are "thin clients" for patterns from a structured space. He closes by saying the most energetic pushback he gets comes from people who reject reductionism for humans but want machines left out, and that "it has to go all the way down".

## Main ideas (plain words)

- **Track intelligence, not life.** He says cognition and intelligence are more useful terms than living versus non-living, and that dead matter and dumb machines are simplifying abstractions; Turing-style models do not tell the whole story even for machines.
- **An assumption, not a result.** Biology students are taught to explain everything at the level of chemistry, where nothing knows anything. He calls that preference an assumption baked into the curriculum, and notes Denis Noble's phrase "privileged level of causation" (the captions spell it "Dennis").
- **Communicate, don't micromanage.** Bioelectric signals let his lab give cells new goals in animal models: growing eyes and limbs, regenerating organs, normalizing tumors caused by human oncogenes, and now work on aging and longevity. He compares it to training dogs and horses without knowing neuroscience.
- **Test, don't assume.** You cannot tell how intelligent a system is by watching; you have to put barriers between it and its goal. That is doable for cells, very hard for something like a solar system.
- **Not exactly random.** Pointing to upcoming work, he says the mathematics guiding processes important at the origin of life is already biased toward gains in learning and causal emergence, while adding that this does not mean a long-term goal or an immense intelligence.
- **Mind and body like math and physics.** His recent proposal: the relationship between mind and body is the same kind of thing as that between mathematics and physics. Patterns from a separate space act as behavioral policies, and new embodiments, from xenobots to simple algorithms, get "free lunches" not paid for by design, selection or training.
- **Latent space.** "Emergence" is too thin an answer; asking why something emerged ends in the math department. He sees cells, organisms, machines and cyborgs as front-end interfaces that host patterns from a structured, explorable space. New kinds of embodiments may draw on very different patterns, so a system that talks like a human should not be assumed to have a human mind. "We facilitate their ingression into the physical world."
- **Closing.** He has thought about these ideas for decades but, per the captions, has been talking about them only "since 2025", once they became actionable in the lab. He says the most energetic pushback comes from the organicist community, which he links to "a scarcity mindset with respect to compassion", and ends by saying we are not physical objects that occasionally receive inspiring patterns: "We are the patterns."

## Why it matters

A plain-language version of his current program for a general, non-specialist audience, and a clear statement of why he refuses to draw a line between living beings and machines. It pairs with the [[2026-10-28-thoughts-as-thinkers-ccs26-keynote|CCS 2026 keynote]], which gives the experimental examples behind the same ideas. Concept: [[platonic-space]].
