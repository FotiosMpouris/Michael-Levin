# Bioelectricity, Aging, and the Future of Medicine (Arjun Khemani)

- **Date published:** 2026-09-21
- **Channel:** Arjun Khemani (not the Levin lab channel)
- **Speakers:** Michael Levin, interviewed by Arjun Khemani
- **Format:** Interview (56 min)
- **Watch:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BWgJPmeW5g0
- **Topics:** [[bioelectricity|Bioelectricity]], [[regeneration|Regeneration]], [[cognition-in-cells|Cognition in cells]], [[mind-body|Mind, body and brain]]

## In one paragraph

An interview focused on aging. Levin separates the root cause of aging from its downstream effects and puts forward his lab's "boredom theory of aging": a collective of cells held together by a shared goal can drift apart once that goal is reached and no new one replaces it. He describes what is known about bioelectric changes with age, how the lab decodes and rewrites pattern memories such as the number of heads in a planarian, a fruit-fly lifespan experiment in progress, and why he expects future medicine to look more like "somatic psychiatry" than chemistry. He closes on the bottlenecks: bioelectric data at scale and imaging that reaches deep tissue.

## Main ideas (plain words)

- **Two kinds of aging theory, and a third.** Standard theories put the cause in physics (accumulating errors) or in evolution (it pays to die at some point). Levin's lab proposes a cause at the level of cognition.
- **What is known about bioelectric change with age.** His lab sees bioelectric differences between aging and non-aging strains of hydra, and changes in human cells as they senesce in vitro. Older cells also resist being shifted to a new voltage. He says it may be both: some information is lost, and cells get worse at acting on it. He stresses this is in vitro and has to be done in vivo.
- **Pattern memory is real memory.** To the question of whether two-headed planarians are just pushed into a new stable state, he answers that pushing a system into a different attractor "is a pretty good description of memory". The lab decoded the bioelectric pattern for the number of heads, rewrote it, and the change persists through further cuts. He says they have not yet decoded head shape well.
- **Cracking the bioelectric code.** Measure bioelectric patterns in health and disease, guess what they tell cells, test by targeted changes, and build computer models. He says the lab started with screens around 1998 and now has computational platforms that predict drugs that repair complex birth defects in animal models.
- **The boredom theory of aging.** Thought experiment: a snake would be fine in an unchanging heaven forever; would a human stay sane for a billion years? In a simulation by Benedikt Hartl and Léo Pio-Lopez in his group, a cell collective built a good frog face, held it, then fell apart, even though the model had no entropic decay and no evolutionary pressure to fail. The goal had simply stopped being compelling.
- **Human data.** Using phylostratigraphy on public gene-expression data, he says some tissues in older people express evolutionarily older genes and the cells stop agreeing on what they are, which he calls atavistic dissociation, a mirror image of development.
- **Regeneration as a refresh.** He suggests planaria may avoid aging partly because they keep tearing themselves in half and regenerating. In their model, inducing damage and regeneration put off aging. He wonders, explicitly off the top of his head, whether evolution plays a similar role for the germline.
- **Bioelectrics and epigenetic reprogramming.** Asked about David Sinclair's work, he bets bioelectrics sits upstream, because in his experience there is usually a bioelectric switch upstream of molecular events, but says nobody knows yet.
- **A test in progress.** If aging blurs the bioelectric pattern, sharpening it might help, as it does for some birth defects. With Mitch McVey at Tufts, the lab has made bioelectrically altered fruit flies and is measuring their lifespan; he had no answer yet at recording time and hoped to show data at an aging conference at Harvard Medical School in early October.
- **Clocks and consciousness.** He says he is not an expert on aging clocks. On consciousness, he says none of what he described needs a theory of consciousness, and he doubts that "unconscious" processes are even coherent, since all our evidence comes from human verbal reports.
- **Future medicine.** Medicine will focus on resetting goal states and communicating with the body's collective intelligence rather than micromanaging receptors. Bottlenecks: high-throughput bioelectric profiling, imaging that reaches deep tissue, and better simulators. He mentions Morphoceuticals, a company he is associated with, developing a bioelectric simulator that updates BETSE, the simulator built by Alexis Pietak (see [[companies-tracker|Companies tracker]]).

## Why it matters

This is the fullest account on this site of the boredom theory of aging, which comes up only briefly in the [[2026-11-05-is-consciousness-popularity-narcis-marincat|Marincat discussion]]. The interview sets out the theory in plain terms, names the evidence for it (a simulation and a reanalysis of public human data) and the evidence still missing (the fruit-fly lifespan study), which makes it easy to see what would confirm or sink it.
