# Solving Rare at Scale

- **Date published:** 2026-11-25
- **Speakers:** Richard Novak (guest talk to the Levin lab on Unravel, the company he says he started five years ago), with discussion with Michael Levin
- **Format:** Guest talk and discussion (47 min)
- **Watch:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xTVa-wsEf5I
- **Topics:** [[ai-and-biology|AI and biology]], [[xenobots-and-anthrobots|Xenobots and anthrobots]]
- **Company:** Unravel Biosciences, see the [[companies-tracker|companies tracker]]. Levin shared this talk on X on 2026-11-25 and called Unravel "the first company our research contributed to"; he wrote that he does not work for it but is involved.

## In one paragraph

Novak presents Unravel, which he calls "the predictable medicine company", and its plan to treat rare diseases many at a time instead of one drug per disease. The idea: start from each patient's own RNA (collected with home nasal swabs), build what he calls a "living molecular twin" of that patient's gene network, and simulate about 40,000 existing molecules against it to find ones predicted to push the patient toward a healthy state. He walks through validation examples, patient stories, a tadpole model, a clinical trial and how patients with different diagnoses group by predicted drug response. He addresses Levin as one of the company's founding academic partners. In the discussion Levin asks whether the platform could pick drugs that steer anthrobots, which he describes as having very different gene expression from the cells they came from.

## Main ideas (plain words)

- The problem as he puts it: there are about 15,000 known rare diseases, a new drug costs a billion dollars or more, and for truly new targets only about 2% of drugs in the clinic succeed. One drug per disease cannot cover that.
- Patients, not diagnoses, respond to drugs. A diagnosis is often a grouping by symptoms. Cells in a dish are only a model of a patient: no organs, no daily rhythm, none of the medications many of these patients take.
- The platform (BioNav; he says the academic version was called NemoCAD when the team was still at Harvard) maps how a drug's effect on gene expression spreads through a gene regulatory network, ranks drugs from therapeutic to harmful, and shows the causal links behind each ranking instead of a bare score.
- Why existing drugs: he says every drug has hundreds of off-target effects, so 40,000 molecules cover a large part of biology, and a predicted route that uses an approved drug is something a clinician can try today. He stresses this is not drug repurposing as an end: what is learned is used to develop new medicines.
- Checks he describes: on organoid data from 10 tuberous sclerosis patients, without being told the disease, the platform put mTOR inhibitors (the standard of care) at the top in about two hours. In a pandemic-era analysis it predicted that some statins would help and others would not, and he says health-record data from a UCSF and Stanford group matched four of five predictions.
- Patient sampling: eight nasal swabs per person over two daily cycles, mailed in a kit that preserves RNA at room temperature. He says they work with undiagnosed patients too and have the largest rare-disease patient RNA dataset (thousands of samples, 130 disorders).
- Patient results he reports: a girl with a severe epilepsy disorder who walked again within seven weeks and has been seizure-free for about six months after her clinician applied predicted drugs (a case report is being prepared), and "about a 97% positive clinical response rate" across clinicians using their data. These are the company's own figures from the talk, not peer-reviewed results.
- Rett syndrome: the platform pointed to a new target and to vorinostat, which he says works through a metabolite. He describes mouse and tadpole results, a young boy treated under his clinician's care, and a clinical trial now running in Colombia with GMP-made drug. He mentions a related program in sea lions with neurotoxin poisoning.
- Grouping across disorders: analysing Pitt-Hopkins patients, they predicted about a quarter would respond badly to vorinostat. Across about 160 patients with 18 more brain disorders they found subgroups that respond differently, and in one disorder (ZTTK) two clinical subgroups with the same mutation that a downstream metabolic gene separates. He says these data will be presented at the American Epilepsy Society meeting the following week, so they are not yet published.
- Levin's question: anthrobots, made from adult human airway cells, differ from those cells in the body in something like 9,000 genes. Could the platform find drugs that steer them to other useful behaviours, treating them as a different kind of being? Novak likens it to chemical reprogramming of stem cells and says the platform needs two expression profiles, one to move away from and one to move toward.
