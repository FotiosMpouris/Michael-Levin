# Mind-Body Interface: an Experimental and Clinical Perspective

- **Date published:** 2026-10-11
- **Speakers:** Michael Levin and Karina Kofman

## In one paragraph

An award talk for a paper with Karina Kofman. Levin places the mind–body problem in a multiscale biological frame; Kofman then reviews clinical cases where mind and brain do not line up the way standard models predict.

## Main ideas (plain words)

- An embryo is not 'one' because of its genes. What makes it one being is the cells' shared commitment to a path through anatomical possibilities; scratch a blastoderm into islands and each island can form its own embryo.
- Top-down control of the body is ancient: a tail grafted where a limb belongs remodels into a limb, and the tail-tip cells become fingers, though no single cell knows what a finger is.
- Memory survives drastic rebuilding of the brain: trained planaria remember after their heads regrow; caterpillar memories are remapped onto a butterfly's completely different body.
- Kofman's clinical review: hydrocephalus cases with very little brain tissue but normal cognition, awareness reported during cardiac arrest, terminal lucidity, psychedelics lowering brain activity while experience intensifies. Presented as case reports, not proof.
- Levin proposes taking mathematical Platonism seriously and extending it: a non-physical space of patterns, from simple math up to kinds of minds, that 'ingress' into bodies, natural or engineered. He frames this as an active empirical research program in his lab.

## Why it matters

This is Levin at his most ambitious: tying lab results on regeneration and memory to medicine and to a new theory of where minds come from. The clinical side matters for anyone thinking about dementia and care.
