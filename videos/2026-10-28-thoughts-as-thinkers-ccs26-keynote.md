## In one paragraph

A keynote on what Levin calls laboratory model systems for inspiration and intuition. He frames it as a "free lunch": the gap between the competencies a system shows and the effort anyone put into it by design, evolution or training. He walks through cases where that gap is large (people with very reduced brain volume and normal IQ, tadpoles that see through an eye on their tail, a turtle on a skateboard, small gene-network models that learn, xenobots, bubble sort), pauses for the room to discuss where such competencies come from, and then gives his answer: a structured, non-physical space of patterns that bodies and machines act as interfaces to. Talks with similar titles appear on two Levin-channel videos on this site; this is a separate recording at a different event, with its own audience questions.

## Main ideas (plain words)

- **Two unpopular claims.** That inspiration has a literally non-physical component, by which he means one not captured by the standard formalisms of physics and computer science, not something mysterious or unstudiable; and that inspirations and the agents they inspire are one continuum, not two kinds of thing.
- **Order is not rare.** Darwin and Paley disagreed on how order arose but agreed it was rare and needed explaining. He says Braitenberg's claim that there is nothing in his vehicles "that we have not put in ourselves" is "a massive assumption", and that Kauffman's "order for free" does not go far enough.
- **Examples of the free lunch.** Tadpoles with an eye on the tail, not wired to the brain, see out of it with no new rounds of selection. Small sets of coupled equations used to model gene networks can learn several ways (the smallest that does Pavlovian conditioning has four nodes); in his lab's results, which he says are in review, learning raises a measure of causal emergence and forcing the network to forget does not undo the gain. He calls this "a free gift from math", there long before replicators and selection.
- **Xenobots.** Built from wild-type frog cells with no editing, they gather loose cells into piles that become the next generation, respond to sound, show calcium-signal patterns that differ from null models about as much as human brain data do, and form at least two kinds of memory that last at least 24 hours. Neurobots (xenobots with neurons added) swim in complex patterns.
- **Bubble sort's side quests.** A few lines of deterministic code, studied for decades, turn out to do things that are nowhere in the algorithm. His view: neither machines nor living things are only what their formal models say.
- **His proposal.** Mathematical truths do not come from the physical world, and mathematicians study them as a structured space. He suggests the same space also holds patterns that behavioral scientists and developmental biologists would recognize as policies and kinds of minds, that bodies are "thin clients" for these patterns, and that five or six people in his lab now work on mapping it experimentally.
- **Thoughts and thinkers.** He describes a continuum from fleeting thoughts to persistent intrusive thoughts to personality alters to whole minds, and closes with "We are the patterns."
- **Questions.** On cherry-picking examples, he says xenobots were "the first darn thing we tried". On societies and solar systems as minds, he says his claims are empirical and need perturbative experiments that are hard at those scales. On consciousness, he says he has no strong claims, then guesses that a truly unconscious system may be a logical impossibility, calling it conjecture. On whether the space is given or made, he thinks mathematics is discovered but that the space can change over time.

## Why it matters

It is a compact, recent statement of the free-lunch and "bodies as interfaces" argument, with new detail on unpublished work (learning and causal emergence in small networks, behavior from mathematical structures given robot bodies) that he flags as early or in review.
