Building Your Compass: a path through idea space and academia (ERA MiniCon talk)

  • Date published: 2026-11-12 (recorded 2026-08-16 at the ERA MiniCon, held before ALIFE 2026 in Waterloo, Canada, per the description)
  • Channel: Emerging Researchers in Artificial Life (ERA), the student chapter of the International Society for Artificial Life
  • YouTube title: "Criticality of a path through idea space and academia - Michael Levin | ERA Minicon 2026". The description gives the talk title as "Building Your Compass: Crafting a Research Agenda for the Long Term".
  • Speakers: Michael Levin (invited career talk), introduced by the session host
  • Format: Talk, not an interview (1 h 10 min)
  • Watch: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=poFvpVF2JyA
  • Topics: Bioelectricity, Regeneration, Xenobots and anthrobots, Platonic space and ingressing minds

In one paragraph

A talk for early-career researchers. Levin was asked to cover both his science and the difficulties behind it, which he says the "sanitized" stories in papers leave out. He traces his path from reading Becker's The Body Electric at 17, through a computer science and biology double major at Tufts, a PhD with Cliff Tabin at Harvard Medical School, a postdoc, his first lab at the Forsyth Institute in 2000 and the move to Tufts in 2009. He then tours the lab's main results (left-right asymmetry, Picasso tadpoles, reading and writing bioelectric pattern memories, two-headed planarians, repairing birth defects, xenobots and anthrobots) and its newest direction, the Platonic space. He ends with frank advice: thick skin, persistence, rejection as routine, and the skills nobody trains you for.

Main ideas (plain words)

  • The questions behind the lab. How different kinds of minds come to be and change, where their properties come from beyond physics, selection, training and design, and how to communicate with truly diverse intelligences, which he says matters for medicine because we need to communicate new goals to body cells.
  • Puzzles the textbooks skip. How many individuals an early embryo holds (he split duck blastoderms as a grad student), people with very little brain tissue and normal cognition, mixed-species planarian stem cells, trophic memory in deer antlers, and memories that survive planarian head regrowth and caterpillar-to-butterfly metamorphosis.
  • Left and right. As a grad student and postdoc he found that the two sides of the early embryo must communicate to set left-right asymmetry, and that this runs on an electrical circuit present from the four-cell stage in frogs. He calls it a stroke of good luck that joined his two interests.
  • Goal-directed development. Scrambled "Picasso" tadpoles still make normal frog faces, which he reads as error-minimization toward a set point rather than hardwired moves. He calls the classroom ban on asking "how does it know?" an assumption, not a result.
  • Reading and writing pattern memories. Voltage dyes show the "electric face". Injecting an ion channel can prompt gut cells to build an eye, recruiting neighbors if too few were hit. Two-headed planarians hold a rewritten, latent pattern with an unchanged genome. Activating the HCN2 channel restored normal brains and learning in frogs with a Notch mutation, according to the talk.
  • The anatomical compiler. His long-term vision: draw the organ or creature you want and have software compute the stimuli to get cells to build it. "This is a communications device," not a 3D printer. He describes Mombot, a robot-scientist platform built with Josh Bongard's lab and Doug Blackiston, that forms hypotheses about the morphogenetic code, stimulates cells, observes and revises, and says language models alone will not reason their way to these answers.
  • Novel beings. Tadpoles that see through an eye on the tail, self-replicating xenobots, and anthrobots that help close a scratch in a layer of human neurons. He asks when the "computational cost" of their abilities was paid, since none were selected for.
  • Platonic space. He says he did not talk about it until 2025 because it was not actionable before. Mathematical structure (his example is a Halley map) is a source of order that is neither history nor physics; his lab's hypothesis is that the same space also holds behavioral competencies, and that even bubble sort shows "ingressions".
  • What he argues against. That genomes dictate form, that molecular biology is the best level for intervention, that memories live only in synapses, sharp lines between life and machine, and physicalism itself.
  • Career advice. A cell biology professor told him in his first week of grad school he should quit. A neuroscientist wrote to his PI that his postdoc plan to express ion channels outside the nervous system made him "dangerously unhinged". For about 15 years he sent out roughly 20 grant applications a year. Impostor syndrome does not leave; you get too busy for it. Public speaking, managing people and money are learned on the job: "Nobody knows the right way to run a lab."

Why it matters

Talks usually present results. This one shows the path and the cost: what he kept quiet about, when, and why. Together with the Science Conversations interview, it is the main source on this site for the order in which his ideas went public, and it is aimed at people deciding whether to work on unconventional problems.
