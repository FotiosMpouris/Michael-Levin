Conversation #1 with Martin Šolc: the law and diverse intelligence

  • Date published: 2026-10-30
  • Speakers: Martin Šolc (postdoc, Charles University Faculty of Law) and Michael Levin
  • Format: Conversation
  • Watch: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CfnBKd_vThc
  • Topics: Mind, body and brain, Cognition in cells

In one paragraph

A legal scholar who works on civil and medical law asks how the law should sort new kinds of entities, such as AIs, cyborgs and animals, and says Levin's TAME framework (a way of measuring cognitive capacity whatever a being is made of) could help. He proposes four "legal recognition levels", from a plain thing up to full legal personhood. Levin argues our who-or-what language is too crude for a coming world of cyborgs and augmented people, that capability and the ability to suffer should be kept apart, and that the trait worth measuring may be the size of a being's "cone of compassion".

Main ideas (plain words)

  • Šolc contrasts common law (built from court precedents) with continental law (rigid categories written by lawmakers), and notes that his country's civil code now treats animals as neither things nor persons.
  • His four levels: a thing protected only by its market value; something without agency whose value exceeds market value (how animals are treated now); narrow legal personhood; and full legal personhood, as humans and corporations have.
  • Levin says the who-or-what split in language is the first thing that has to change, and suggests something like a graded number for how much of a "who" something is.
  • He expects neighbors with brain prosthetics, people with new senses, and linked groups, so a machine-or-human test won't survive the next 10 to 20 years.
  • He separates agency and responsibility from the capacity to suffer: a very capable system might carry responsibilities, while a baby rabbit mostly needs protections.
  • He proposes judging membership by care for others (a "cone of compassion") rather than raw intelligence, and links it to cells that share stress signals so that a neighbor's problem becomes theirs.
  • He notes that developmental biology textbooks don't define what an embryo is, and that his lab is building tools to detect where one agent ends and another begins.
